The question I couldn’t skip

Module 1 — the self as something to be examined

I joined this term three weeks late. While everyone else already had a seat, a group, and a rhythm, I was still trying to find where I fit. That moment made me ask a question I had never fully considered before: if I remove the school I came from and the class I have not joined yet, who am I left with? This question mattered because it pushed me to look inward and realize that identity is not something I simply inherit. It is something I discover, examine, and continue to shape.

Socrates reminded me that the first step toward understanding myself is to question myself honestly. Descartes taught me that even in doubt, I can still affirm my existence because I am thinking. Locke helped me see that memory connects my past self to my present self, which means identity is built through experience. Hume challenged that idea by showing that the self is not one fixed essence, but a changing collection of thoughts, perceptions, and memories. Together, these ideas taught me that understanding the self is not about finding one final answer. It is about learning how I think, remember, and grow.

The me that other people hold

Module 2 — the self in society and culture

On my first day as the late arrival, I saw how much of “me” was being shaped by other people. Cooley’s looking-glass self reminded me that I am influenced by the image I think others have of me. Mead explained that I learn my identity through my relationships with other people. Goffman added that I also perform different versions of myself in different situations, and that is not a sign of dishonesty; it is part of how I adapt and grow. My classmates, my family, and even my code editor each reveal a different side of who I am.

This is also where I learned that my identity is not only personal; it is also social and cultural. My family, my community, and my country gave me my first language for understanding who I am. I can carry what is valuable to me and question the parts that no longer serve me. That balance is part of maturity.

My hometown is Davao, and it shaped the way I move through life. Part of me also misses Banganga, my parents’ hometown, because it remains a place of memory and belonging. Even when I am not there, it still influences who I am. Programming, hiking, and poetry are the three things that help me understand myself more clearly: programming teaches me discipline, hiking teaches me patience, and poetry gives me language for what I feel.

Taking and managing myself

Module 3 — three lessons

Lesson 1: Learning to be a better student

Being a student is a skill, not a label. I learned to treat how I study as something I can improve: planning my time, finding the methods that work for me, and asking for help early. This mattered a lot for someone catching up after joining three weeks late.

Lesson 2: Setting goals for success (SMART plan)

A wish becomes a plan when it is SMART: Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant, and Time-bound. “Get better at programming” is a wish. “Finish one small project and review it by the end of the month” is a plan I can check.

Lesson 3: Taking charge of oneself

The last lesson tied the module together: I am responsible for my own choices, habits, and direction. Nobody else can do the work of becoming who I want to be, but I can build the discipline to do it.

A short poem

The answer, in verse

I came in late, the door half closed, a stranger to the room. I asked the walls and asked the mirror who I was, and when. They said: you are the one who asks, the memory, the doubt, the face that others hand you back, the self you work out loud. So here I sit, no longer late, still writing who I am.

What I learned, in one paragraph

Across the three modules, I learned that the self is not fixed, but formed through reflection, relationships, and choice. Philosophers taught me to question who I am, social thinkers showed me that other people shape me, and the final module taught me to take responsibility for my own growth. I am not one fixed identity; I am a growing mix of memory, values, habits, and decisions. I apply this by reviewing my own actions, choosing the people who help me grow, and setting clearer goals for my studies and my future. Being a late arrival taught me that where I begin does not define where I end. Who am I? I am still becoming, and I am learning to become more intentional about the life I want to build.
